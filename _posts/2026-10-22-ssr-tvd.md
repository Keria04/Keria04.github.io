## Quick Reference

**When to revisit:** How can low-resolution time-varying volumes be upscaled while preserving spatial detail and temporal coherence?

**Key takeaway:** SSR-TVD learns a fourfold-per-axis spatial upscaling of 3D time-varying scalar volumes, using a generator trained with spatial and temporal discriminators plus content and feature losses. Across the reported datasets and evaluation settings, it generally improves reconstruction and rendering quality over bicubic interpolation and a CNN baseline, but its performance depends on the data distribution and training coverage.

## Problem

Scientific simulations produce sequences of 3D scalar volumes whose storage and input/output costs can be substantial. The task is **spatial super-resolution (SSR) of time-varying data (TVD)**: recover a high-resolution volume at each available time step from its low-resolution counterpart, without changing the number of time steps. For example, a sequence of $$128^3$$ volumes can be mapped to $$512^3$$ volumes. A fourfold increase along each spatial axis produces 64 times as many voxels per volume (Section 1).

The introduction presents three potential uses, which should not be confused with identical experiments:

1. **Storage-oriented reconstruction:** downsample volumes generated during a high-resolution simulation, save the smaller data, and reconstruct them during postprocessing. This can serve a compression-and-reconstruction workflow, including an *in situ* data-processing setting.
2. **Reduced-resolution simulation after initial high-resolution steps:** run some early steps at high resolution to provide training examples, then simulate subsequent steps at lower resolution and use the trained model for reconstruction. This is a proposed way to reduce simulation resources, not proof that every high-resolution physical detail can be recovered.
3. **Denser temporal sampling under a storage budget:** save smaller volumes at more time steps (the authors illustrate saving every tenth step instead of every hundredth), then upscale the saved volumes spatially. This improves the potential temporal sampling density of saved outputs; SSR-TVD itself does **not** synthesize missing time steps.

Two difficulties distinguish this problem from single-volume interpolation. First, reconstructed volumes must form a temporally coherent sequence; good-looking individual frames may still change implausibly across time. Second, fixed interpolation kernels may blur localized structures or introduce rendering artifacts, so voxel-level agreement alone does not capture all aspects of visual quality (Section 1).

Prior work had addressed scalar-volume upscaling with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), spatial super-resolution of vector fields, and temporally coherent fluid super-resolution using additional motion-related quantities. SSR-TVD asks whether a generator can learn spatial reconstruction of time-varying volumes while receiving an explicit temporal training signal **without** an additional velocity, vorticity, or optical-flow estimation model (Sections 1–2).

## Core Idea

Use one **fully convolutional generator** $$G$$ to reconstruct each low-resolution volume, but train it against two different views of realism (Fig. 1b):

- The **spatial discriminator** $$D_s$$ distinguishes real and generated *individual* high-resolution volumes.
- The **temporal discriminator** $$D_t$$ distinguishes real and generated *triplets of consecutive* high-resolution volumes.

Neither discriminator is needed during inference. Unlike a recurrent generator that explicitly carries temporal state, $$G$$ processes one volume at a time; temporal relationships influence its learned parameters through adversarial training on consecutive outputs. This encourages temporal coherence but does not mathematically guarantee it.

Three complementary loss terms guide $$G$$: an adversarial term favoring realistic volumes and sequences, a content term enforcing agreement with corresponding ground-truth values, and a feature term matching internal discriminator representations at multiple layers. The approach differs from ordinary bicubic upsampling because it learns a data-dependent reconstruction, and differs from the paper's CNN-only baseline by using adversarial and feature-based supervision (Sections 2–3).

## Method or Argument

### Training data, inference task, and notation

Let $$V_i^L$$ be a low-resolution volume at time step $$i$$, and $$V_i^H$$ its corresponding high-resolution ground truth (GT). The generator predicts

$$
\widehat V_i^H = G(V_i^L).
$$

The paper defines the sets $$V_s^L=\{V_1^L,\ldots,V_n^L\}$$ and $$V_s^H=\{V_1^H,\ldots,V_n^H\}$$ for individual volumes. Its temporal samples are overlapping triplets such as $$(V_{i-1}^L,V_i^L,V_{i+1}^L)$$ and their high-resolution counterparts (Section 3.1).

In the reported setup, high-resolution volumes from **earlier time steps** provide paired training data; bicubic downsampling by a factor of four along each spatial dimension creates the low-resolution inputs. Volumes at **later time steps** are reconstructed with the trained generator (Fig. 1a; Section 4.1). The paper explicitly discusses experiments using the first 40% of time steps for training (Section 4.2). This is temporal extrapolation to later steps within a dataset, not an evaluation of unrestricted transfer to unrelated simulations.

For same-variable inference, training and inference use the same scalar variable at different time steps. For **different-variable inference**, a model trained on variable $$X$$ is applied to another variable $$Y$$ from the *same dataset*. It still performs spatial upscaling on $$Y$$; it is not trained to translate values from $$X$$ into $$Y$$ (Section 4.2; Figs. 5 and 8).

### Generator: feature extraction followed by learned upsampling

The generator has seven **identical blocks (IBs)** and two **deconvolution/transposed-convolution (DeConv)** layers (Fig. 2a; Table 1):

1. Three IBs process the low-resolution input without changing spatial size.
2. The first DeConv doubles each spatial dimension; two further IBs refine the resulting features.
3. The second DeConv doubles each dimension again; two final IBs produce and refine the full-resolution output.

An IB combines a multi-convolution feature path (with spectral normalization, instance normalization, and activation) and a shorter convolutional path joined through a skip connection (Fig. 2c). Connections across IBs help information flow through the generator. These modules are intended to support feature learning and gradient propagation while DeConv performs the spatial expansion. Table 1 shows channels increasing from 1 through 16, 64, and 128 at low resolution, then decreasing toward a final one-channel volume. The last activation is $$\tanh$$, producing values in $$[-1,1]$$.

Because the generator is **fully convolutional**, its architecture does not require a fixed input width, height, or depth. This allows training on cropped subvolumes and inference on larger volumes, subject to memory and boundary-handling constraints. The upscaling factor is nevertheless fixed by the two $$\times2$$ DeConv stages (Section 4.1).

### Discriminators: single-volume and three-volume inputs

Both discriminators use the same *architecture* but have separate learned weights (Fig. 2b; Section 3.2). Each contains five convolutional layers with output-channel counts 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1. Spectral normalization (SN) and Leaky ReLU ($$\alpha=0.2$$) are used in the discriminative feature extractor. Strided convolution replaces pooling; the last convolution produces a $$1\times1\times1$$ score for the described training configuration and has no final activation.

$$D_s$$ examines one volume. $$D_t$$ examines three consecutive volumes, concatenated as its input. Consequently, $$D_t$$ can learn features of local changes across time without an explicit optical-flow or recurrent-network module. Three steps are chosen instead of five or more to limit computational cost (Section 3). Its score evaluates the realism of a short sequence; it is not a measured motion vector or a guarantee of physically correct dynamics.

### Losses: three complementary constraints

**Adversarial loss, Eqs. (1)–(3).** The paper follows a least-squares GAN formulation: discriminators are trained toward a score of 1 for real input and 0 for generated input, while the generator tries to make their scores on generated input approach 1. In compact form, with expectations over the appropriate single-volume and triplet training samples,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}} =
\mathbb E[(D_s(G(V_i^L))-1)^2]
+\mathbb E[(D_t(\widehat{\mathbf V}_{i-1:i+1}^H)-1)^2],
$$

where $$\widehat{\mathbf V}_{i-1:i+1}^H$$ denotes the three individually generated high-resolution volumes grouped as one input to $$D_t$$. The spatial discriminator minimizes the average of $$(D_s(V_i^H)-1)^2$$ on real data and $$D_s(G(V_i^L))^2$$ on generated data; $$D_t$$ uses the analogous two terms for real and generated triplets. Thus the discriminators and generator optimize competing, not identical, objectives.

**Content loss, Eq. (4).** For aligned low-/high-resolution pairs, the paper writes an L2-distance objective:

$$
\mathcal L_{L2}=\mathbb E\!\left[\left\|G(V_i^L)-V_i^H\right\|_2\right].
$$

This ties a generated volume to its particular GT volume; a plausible but mismatched shape is not sufficient. **Notation matters:** the displayed Eq. (4) uses an L2 *norm*, without an explicit square. The accompanying prose refers to mean-squared-error-style reconstruction, so theoretical claims requiring a *squared* L2 objective should be stated conditionally rather than attributed to the displayed equation.

**Feature loss, Eq. (5).** The method also compares the intermediate convolutional representations $$F^k$$ extracted from a real high-resolution sample and its generated counterpart:

$$
\mathcal L_F = \mathbb E\!\left[
\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N_k}
\left\|F^k(V_i^H)-F^k(G(V_i^L))\right\|_2
\right].
$$

Here $$T$$ denotes the relevant discriminator's convolution-layer count and $$N_k$$ the number of elements at layer $$k$$. Multiple layers offer comparisons at different feature scales; division by $$N_k$$ adjusts for representation size, not a guarantee of equal weighting across layers. Gradients through those features guide the generator. This resembles perceptual or feature-matching losses but does not itself establish physical correctness.

The full generator objective is (Eq. 6)

$$
\mathcal L_G = \lambda_1\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}} +
\lambda_2\mathcal L_{L2} + \lambda_3\mathcal L_F,
$$

with empirically selected weights $$\lambda_1=10^{-3}$$, $$\lambda_2=1$$, and $$\lambda_3=5\times10^{-2}$$ (Section 4.1). The three losses constrain different aspects of the same reconstruction rather than defining three independent outputs.

### Alternating optimization and implementation

Algorithm 1 loops over training epochs and center time steps $$i=2,\ldots,n-1$$, where a three-volume window $$(i-1,i,i+1)$$ is available. For each sampled position, it updates $$D_s$$ using Eq. (2), then $$D_t$$ using Eq. (3), and finally $$G$$ using the complete Eq. (6). During each network's update, the objective is to change that network's parameters; the other networks provide the current inputs or gradients needed for its loss.

The reported settings are $$n_{D_s}=2$$, $$n_{D_t}=2$$, and $$n_G=1$$ updates per iteration. The authors use Adam with $$\beta_1=0.5$$, $$\beta_2=0.999$$, a learning rate of $$4\times10^{-4}$$ for each discriminator and $$10^{-4}$$ for the generator, and a mini-batch size of one (Section 4.1). Separate discriminator and generator learning rates instantiate the **two time-scale update rule (TTUR)**; update frequency and learning rate are separate choices. Spectral normalization is used in both the generator and discriminators to help stabilize adversarial learning (Section 3.3).

Four low-resolution subvolumes and their high-resolution counterparts are randomly cropped at each time step in each epoch. Low-resolution inputs are scaled to $$[0,1]$$ and high-resolution targets to $$[-1,1]$$, consistent with the generator's final $$\tanh$$. Training used one NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU (Section 4.1). Large-volume inference may be divided into subvolumes and assembled after reconstruction (Section 4.3).

### Data and evaluation setup

Table 2 reports six time-varying dataset families, with eleven dataset-variable configurations:

| Dataset family | Variables listed | High-resolution spatial dimensions | Time steps |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Argon bubble | One listed configuration | $$640\times256\times256$$ | 160 |
| Combustion | HR, MF, YOH | $$240\times360\times60$$ | 100 |
| Five jets | One listed configuration | $$128^3$$ | 100 |
| Hurricane | One listed configuration | $$500\times500\times100$$ | 48 |
| Ionization | H, H+, He, He+ | $$600\times248\times248$$ | 100 |
| Vortex | One listed configuration | $$128^3$$ | 90 |

The main upscaling baselines are **bicubic interpolation (BI)** and a **CNN-only post-upsampling** model; Fig. 9 additionally compares against a lossy compression (LC) scheme. The authors keep lighting, viewpoint, and transfer function fixed across rendered comparisons *within each dataset*. They evaluate distinct outcomes: peak signal-to-noise ratio (**PSNR**) on volume values, structural similarity (**SSIM**) on rendered images, isosurface similarity (**IS**) on extracted structures, and mean opinion score (**MOS**) for human-rated resemblance to GT (Section 4.2). These measures address different properties and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Evidence and Results

### 1. Does SSR-TVD improve numerical and rendered-image agreement?

**Test and conditions:** Compare SSR-TVD with BI and CNN on the reported time-varying datasets, using PSNR for reconstructed volumes and SSIM for their volume-rendering images. Fig. 3 plots values over time for four configurations; Table 3 reports sequence averages. The CNN baseline uses a post-upsampling framework (Section 4.2).

| Dataset (variable) | PSNR: BI / CNN / SSR-TVD (dB) | SSIM: BI / CNN / SSR-TVD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Five jets | 30.724 / 28.476 / **40.145** | 0.809 / 0.840 / **0.867** |
| Ionization (H+) | 16.000 / 26.663 / **38.584** | 0.898 / 0.745 / **0.902** |
| Ionization (He+) | 17.116 / 24.933 / **34.738** | 0.869 / 0.708 / **0.870** |
| Vortex | 21.926 / 37.337 / **40.795** | 0.892 / 0.861 / **0.923** |

**Paper finding:** SSR-TVD has the highest average PSNR and SSIM in all four configurations in Table 3, although the SSIM margin over BI is minimal for ionization (He+). The PSNR curves for five jets and vortex decline at later time steps; the authors relate this to training on early time steps only and report that more training steps can improve later predictions. SSR-TVD's stored model is 60.6 MB versus 20.3 MB for the CNN baseline (Fig. 3; Table 3; Section 4.2).

**Supported conclusion:** Better volume-value agreement and rendered-image similarity under these datasets, baselines, and settings. These scores alone do not directly measure the physical correctness of reconstructed dynamics or provide a separate quantified temporal-coherence test. Table 3 explicitly averages over the entire volume sequences; the paper states that, unless otherwise noted, the *displayed synthesized visualization examples* are inference results not observed during training (Section 4.2).

### 2. Are spatial features preserved, including across variables?

**Test and conditions:** Compare volume-rendered appearances against GT for same-variable inference (Fig. 4) and different-variable inference (Fig. 5). Compare extracted isosurfaces at selected time steps and isovalues (Figs. 6–8), with average IS values for two configurations in Table 4. The cross-variable cases transfer a trained upscaler to another variable within the same simulation family, not across arbitrary unrelated datasets.

**Paper finding:** Visual comparisons show finer local structures or fewer artifacts for SSR-TVD in several combustion, five jets, hurricane, and ionization examples. For ionization (He+) at isovalue $$-0.803$$, Table 4 reports average IS of **0.76** for SSR-TVD versus **0.43** for BI; for vortex at isovalue $$0.1$$, the values are **0.92** versus **0.85**. Different-variable examples include combustion MF to YOH and ionization H to He, for which the figures report improvements over BI (Figs. 5, 8).

**Supported conclusion:** Under the illustrated transfer functions, isovalues, and dataset-specific variable pairings, SSR-TVD can preserve some structures better than BI. Visual examples and selected isosurfaces do not establish universal feature preservation for all structures or variable transfers.

### 3. Do people perceive the reconstructions as closer to GT?

**Test and conditions:** Ten graduate students judged the similarity of reconstruction-based rendering images to GT on a 1–5 scale. The study used four datasets, six time steps per dataset, three compared methods, and two image types (volume rendering and isosurface rendering), giving **144 image pairs** in total. Participants could revisit scores and were asked to consider overall impression, shifts, local color/shape, and noise (Section 4.2).

**Paper finding:** SSR-TVD has the highest MOS across the four datasets and both rendering types in Table 5. For combustion (HR), volume-rendering MOS is **3.57** for SSR-TVD versus **2.42** for BI and **2.83** for CNN; isosurface-rendering MOS is **3.90** versus **2.05** and **3.05**, respectively.

**Supported conclusion:** These participants generally rated SSR-TVD's images as more similar to the corresponding GT images in this study. MOS is a perceptual assessment of rendered images, not a direct measurement of numerical field accuracy, simulation fidelity, or downstream scientific decisions.

### 4. How does reconstruction compare with lossy compression?

**Test and conditions:** Fig. 9 compares combustion isosurfaces produced by SSR-TVD and a lossy compression (LC) scheme under **two different matched conditions**: the same compression ratio (**14.98**) and the same PSNR (**30 dB**). For the compression-ratio comparison, the authors include SSR-TVD's **60.6 MB model** in the storage accounting, along with 60 reconstructed time steps and their saved low-resolution volumes (Section 4.2).

**Paper finding:** Under the matched compression ratio, the LC example has less smooth isosurfaces in some regions. Under matched PSNR, LC loses some illustrated structural details and introduces more artifacts than SSR-TVD (Fig. 9).

**Supported conclusion:** This is evidence of better isosurface appearance for the selected combustion cases under two controlled comparison conditions. Equal compression ratio and equal PSNR are **separate comparisons**, not simultaneous constraints; the figure does not establish superiority across every scientific dataset, compressor, or operating regime.

## Limitations

**Paper-stated limitations and operational boundaries (Section 4.3):**

- **Training and inference costs are substantial.** With 40% training samples and $$16^3$$ cropped low-resolution subvolumes, the authors report **16 hours** for vortex (400 epochs) and **48 hours** for hurricane (500 epochs). Across the stated cases, whole-sequence inference ranges from about **10 minutes** (vortex) to **8 hours** (ionization). Large volumes need tiled inference because of GPU memory limits. Full convolution removes a fixed-input-size restriction, not computation or memory costs.
- **Transfer functions are excluded from training.** The generator learns from scalar values; color, opacity, and lighting are determined afterward. Including a transfer function might improve appearance or SSIM for some visualization settings, but the authors note the added dependence on user choices and the challenge of choosing and integrating suitable functions.
- **The training interval is restricted to the early segment.** The study considers training on the initial portion of each sequence; later steps can have lower PSNR. The authors propose investigating which temporal segment is most representative, possibly depending on the dataset.
- **Fine-detail reconstruction fails for some distributions.** For **argon bubble**, both BI and SSR-TVD fail to recover enough detail (Fig. 10). The authors *suspect* difficulty in learning complex distributions: Fig. 11 contrasts its Gaussian/long-tail mixture with the more Gaussian-like distributions of five jets and vortex. This histogram comparison supports a hypothesis; it does not establish a unique causal explanation for failure.

The reported comparisons support reconstruction and visualization quality under the stated settings. They should not be enlarged into a guarantee of physical fidelity, arbitrarily long-range temporal coherence, or equivalent accuracy for unseen simulation families. The cited online appendix contains additional network/hyperparameter analysis, but that material is outside the version consulted here.

## Relation

- [**TSR-TVD: Temporal Super-Resolution for Time-Varying Data Analysis and Visualization**](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2019.2934255) (Han and Wang, 2020; paper Ref. [10]) also uses a generative framework for time-varying volumes. **Difference:** TSR-TVD synthesizes intermediate *time steps* with a temporal component including ConvLSTM layers; SSR-TVD increases the *spatial* resolution at existing time steps and uses a temporal discriminator. They address different missing-resolution dimensions (Section 2).
- [**tempoGAN: A Temporally Coherent, Volumetric GAN for Super-Resolution Fluid Flow**](https://doi.org/10.1145/3197517.3201304) (Xie et al., 2018; Ref. [44]) is directly comparable because it already uses adversarial learning and a temporal discriminator for coherent volumetric super-resolution. **Difference emphasized by SSR-TVD:** tempoGAN can use additional velocity/vorticity information, whereas SSR-TVD is designed to train without those auxiliary fields. SSR-TVD's novelty should not be simplified to “the first temporal discriminator for volume super-resolution” (Section 2).
- [**SSR-VFD: Spatial Super-Resolution for Vector Field Data Analysis and Visualization**](https://hdl.handle.net/1783.1/142285) (Guo et al., 2020; Ref. [7]) also reconstructs high-resolution 3D field data. **Difference:** it targets vector fields and emphasizes spatial reconstruction, while SSR-TVD targets time-varying scalar volumes and adds an explicit temporal discriminator (Section 2).

## My Learning Notes

### What is in situ visualization, and how does it relate to postprocessing?

**First encountered term; additional explanation grounded in the paper's use (Abstract; Section 1).** *In situ visualization* refers broadly to analyzing or processing simulation data while a simulation is running, rather than relying entirely on writing full outputs to disk for later use. In SSR-TVD's proposed workflow, selected volumetric data are downsampled during simulation, stored at reduced size, and spatially upscaled **during postprocessing**. Therefore, the in situ part is not necessarily the final high-resolution rendering. It is important to separate (a) reducing storage after high-resolution simulation output, (b) actually running later simulation steps at lower spatial resolution, and (c) using smaller saved volumes to afford more frequent temporal samples. These are three related motivations, not the same operation.

### Why does bicubic downsampling plus content loss not imply bicubic upsampling?

**Question raised during reading; mathematical clarification.** Since the paper constructs $$V^L=D_{\mathrm{bicubic}}(V^H)$$ and trains $$G$$ against the original $$V^H$$, it is reasonable to ask whether a generator trained *only* with content loss must become a bicubic interpolator.

The training objective instead seeks a data-dependent approximate inverse:

$$
G^*\in\arg\min_G\mathbb E\left[
\left\|G(D_{\mathrm{bicubic}}(V^H))-V^H\right\|_2
\right].
$$

Bicubic **downsampling** discards information and generally is not one-to-one; bicubic **upsampling** is a fixed interpolation rule, not its exact inverse. Nothing in the objective requires $$G^*=U_{\mathrm{bicubic}}$$. The learned predictor can exploit recurring structures in training data, though its reconstruction remains uncertain when different high-resolution details produce similar low-resolution inputs.

**Conditional mathematical detail:** if the loss were *squared* L2 (mean squared error), its unconstrained optimal prediction for an ambiguous input would be the conditional mean, which can average incompatible details and look smooth. For the *unsquared* L2 norm shown in Eq. (4), the corresponding unconstrained optimum is a conditional geometric median. Thus, possible smoothing is a useful intuition, **not** a proof that content-only training mathematically reduces to bicubic interpolation. A content-only CNN could still outperform BI under suitable data and training conditions.

### Why match intermediate discriminator features instead of using only content and adversarial loss?

**Selected explanation of Eq. (5).** Content loss compares aligned reconstructed and GT volume values. Adversarial loss uses a discriminator's **final real/fake score**. Feature loss instead compares the **intermediate convolutional representations** produced for the real and reconstructed samples at corresponding layers. Its $$1/N_k$$ factors scale layer contributions by feature-element count, while summation over several layers constrains features at different depths.

A useful mental model is to pass both volumes through the same learned feature extractor and compare like with like at each layer. The differences backpropagate to $$G$$, supplying more detailed signals than a final scalar score alone. These learned features need not have an explicit physical interpretation. The three losses are complementary: numerical agreement, adversarial realism, and intermediate feature agreement.
